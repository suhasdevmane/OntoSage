\begin{abstract}
Smart buildings are becoming pervasive in commercial and civic infrastructure, generating high-fidelity sensor data that can support energy efficiency, occupant comfort, and regulatory compliance. Despite this potential, effective Human-Building Interaction (HBI) is limited by technical barriers: accessing building telemetry typically requires specialised knowledge of sensor taxonomies, database schemas, and query languages, excluding most stakeholders from meaningful engagement. Recent Large Language Model (LLM) approaches have shown promise, but struggle with hallucinations and fail to generalise across heterogeneous building infrastructures.

In this work, we describe the iterative development of OntoSage, a semantic, agentic conversational AI framework for smart buildings. We started with a formative study using staged prompting to understand stakeholder intentions, which revealed a critical ``context gap'': when users lack technical knowledge, they ask fundamentally different questions---focusing on reasoning and well-being rather than raw telemetry values. Learning from this finding, we designed a system that grounds natural language in a Brick Schema knowledge graph and orchestrates retrieval, analytics, and visualisation through specialised agents. To evaluate portability, we deployed OntoSage at a live academic testbed and adapted it to two diverse synthetic building environments (a commercial office and a data centre model), then conducted a user study.

Our formative study with 81 participants across nine stakeholder roles produced 5{,}916 natural-language questions across four elicitation stages, establishing a multi-dimensional query taxonomy and revealing strong stage effects (Kruskal-Wallis $H=51.1$, $p < 10^{-10}$). A subsequent evaluation deployment with 15 users at an academic testbed achieved 98.3\% task completion, a mean System Usability Scale (SUS) score of 84.5 (``excellent'' threshold), and 93.3\% adoption intent. Cross-building adaptation required 66--68 person-hours with zero changes to analytics or frontend layers. We share lessons learned, error analysis, and implications for scalable, semantic conversational AI in the built environment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Smart buildings are becoming increasingly instrumented, with dense sensor networks generating continuous telemetry on indoor air quality, thermal comfort, energy consumption, and occupancy. This data holds the promise of optimising building performance and occupant well-being. Owing to the richness of this data, modern Building Management Systems (BMS) can enable wide-area monitoring of environmental states without manual inspection. However, building telemetry is rarely actioned into accessible insights: it remains locked behind complex dashboards that primarily surface raw data points rather than supporting explanation, diagnosis, and reasoning.

This trend is slowly changing with the emergence of Large Language Model (LLM)-driven conversational interfaces for buildings. Recent systems such as BuildingGPT \cite{du2025} and SPARA \cite{Karjou_2025} have demonstrated the potential of combining LLMs with building data. In general, these LLM-powered systems require building-specific configuration and prompt engineering, and do not easily generalise across the heterogeneous infrastructure of different buildings. Moreover, LLMs are prone to hallucination when generating database queries, and most existing systems function as ``copilots'' for specific software tools rather than as holistic interfaces for raw building telemetry. This is obviously impractical for the long-tailed distribution of stakeholder needs in diverse built environments \cite{galanos2023,shi2025}.

To flexibly adapt to diverse stakeholders, we developed OntoSage, a semantic, agentic conversational AI framework that grounds natural language queries in a Brick Schema knowledge graph and orchestrates retrieval, analytics, and visualisation through specialised agents. Rather than requiring users to know sensor names, database schemas, or query languages, OntoSage enables \emph{zero-knowledge} interaction: in this work, the term refers to stakeholders engaging with the system without any prior knowledge of the building's underlying sensor taxonomies, spatial UUIDs, or database schemas---distinct from the cryptographic notion of zero-knowledge proofs. Stakeholders can ask high-level questions (e.g., ``Is it stuffy?'', ``Are we meeting ASHRAE 55?'') and the system translates these into executable telemetry queries. However, it is unclear how users feel about such systems in practice, what questions they would formulate, and what errors and challenges emerge. We focus on four main research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1 (Intentions)}: What do diverse building stakeholders naturally ask when they lack technical knowledge of the underlying systems, and how does revealing technical context change their questions?
    \item \textbf{RQ2 (Performance)}: How effectively does a semantically-grounded agentic architecture translate zero-knowledge stakeholder intentions into accurate telemetry queries, and what task completion rates, usability, and cognitive load does it achieve across diverse stakeholder roles?
    \item \textbf{RQ3 (Applications)}: How do different stakeholder roles use conversational building AI, and what question types and complexity levels do they prioritise?
    \item \textbf{RQ4 (Generalisability)}: How much engineering effort is required to adapt the system across heterogeneous building infrastructures?
\end{itemize}

To investigate these questions, we iteratively developed OntoSage, a full-stack conversational AI system for smart buildings. With an early prototype, we conducted a formative study using a staged prompting methodology with 81 participants to map stakeholder intentions and identify design requirements (SREC approval \textbf{COMSC/Ethics/2025/044b}). Learning from the formative findings---particularly the ``context gap'' between what users want to ask and what technical systems support---we developed an improved system architecture grounded in semantic standards. To evaluate portability, we deployed OntoSage at a live academic testbed (Building~A) and adapted it to two diverse synthetic building environments (a commercial office and a data centre), then conducted a 15-participant evaluation study to assess usability, task completion, and adoption potential.

More specifically, OntoSage makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A \emph{staged prompting methodology} and resulting multi-dimensional taxonomy of 5{,}916 stakeholder questions that reveals how technical context shapes user intentions (the ``context gap'').
    \item A \emph{semantic agentic architecture} combining a Brick Schema knowledge graph with LangGraph-orchestrated agents for intent classification, SPARQL generation, time-series retrieval, sandboxed analytics, and visualisation.
    \item A \emph{cross-building adaptation workflow} (T0--T3) that localises change to ontology mapping and database connectors, requiring 66--68 person-hours---approximately \textbf{75\% less} than traditional approaches---with zero analytics or frontend changes.
    \item \emph{Empirical evaluation} with 15 real users achieving 98.3\% task completion, mean SUS 84.5, and 93.3\% adoption intent, with detailed error analysis and design implications.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related-work} reviews related work and defines zero-knowledge interaction. Section~\ref{sec:formative-study} presents the formative study including topic prioritisation and role-based analysis. Section~\ref{sec:system} details the OntoSage system architecture with representative use cases. Section~\ref{sec:deployment} describes the evaluation deployment. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports quantitative results by research question. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses design principles, error analysis, and perceived value. Section~\ref{sec:future-work} addresses limitations and future directions. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

Our work sits at the intersection of Human-Building Interaction, conversational AI for the built environment, and semantic interoperability standards. We review the most relevant work and identify the gaps our system addresses.

\subsection{Human-Building Interaction and Data Accessibility}

There is a substantial HBI literature focused on presenting building data to stakeholders to support behavioural change, operational efficiency, and regulatory compliance. Dashboard-centric Building Management Systems provide high-fidelity views for trained operators but impose high cognitive load on non-expert stakeholders by requiring knowledge of sensor taxonomies, data schemas, and domain-specific terminology \cite{Häikiö2020}. Simplified mobile and public-display interfaces broaden access but typically oversimplify telemetry---reducing multi-dimensional environmental quality to single-number comfort scores---and fail to support the composite, multi-objective queries (e.g., ``Is this building meeting both ASHRAE~55 and BREEAM targets?'') that facility managers and building owners routinely need \cite{ma2023}.

The ``actionability'' problem is well documented: raw telemetry data rarely drives behavioural change unless it is contextualised within the user's current goal and decision horizon \cite{Nabavi2023}. This has motivated exploratory work on occupant-facing dashboards with narrative annotations, personalised energy feedback, and gamified engagement mechanisms---but these interventions are narrowly focused on energy or thermal comfort and do not generalise to the full range of stakeholder intentions. Our formative study is the first large-scale corpus study (5{,}916 questions across nine roles) that systematically maps what stakeholders \emph{actually want to ask} across all building domains and elicitation contexts.

A key finding from the HBI literature is the ``intention-capability gap'': what users want to ask and what the system can answer are structurally misaligned, particularly for non-expert users \cite{galanos2023}. OntoSage directly addresses this gap through a semantic translation layer that maps vague, high-level intents into ontology-grounded queries, without requiring users to know sensor identifiers or query languages.

\subsection{Conversational AI in the Built Environment}

The paradigm of interacting with smart environments has shifted from rigid, rule-based command systems to flexible, LLM-driven interfaces. Early conversational approaches relied on template-based slot filling and finite-state dialogue managers, which lacked flexibility for complex, multi-turn queries. Recent systems integrate LLMs with domain-specific retrieval backends to bridge natural language and structured building data.

BuildingGPT \cite{du2025} applies a Vector-Graph RAG architecture---combining Brick Schema graph traversal with dense vector retrieval---to answer natural language questions over building metadata. SPARA \cite{Karjou_2025} similarly employs RAG-enhanced LLM prompting as an energy efficiency advisor, demonstrating improved response accuracy over pure LLM baselines for standards-related queries. Both systems represent significant progress, but they share two limitations: (1) they target metadata discovery rather than live telemetry retrieval, and (2) their evaluations focus on technical accuracy metrics (query match, answer faithfulness) rather than end-to-end user experience with non-expert stakeholders.

On the multi-agent side, Text2BIM and Architext apply orchestrated LLM pipelines to generative design tasks \cite{helmi2025,du2024}. These systems demonstrate the power of agent decomposition for complex, multi-step tasks---but they target design-time rather than operational building management. The application of agentic workflows to live telemetry retrieval, sandboxed analytics, and standards compliance checking remains under-explored \cite{shi2025}.

A notable gap across this literature is evaluation rigour. Most systems report technical metrics (BLEU score, top-k retrieval accuracy, SQL execution success rate) without assessing whether real users---particularly non-expert occupants and guests---can accomplish their information goals. OntoSage addresses this by conducting a 15-participant evaluation using validated instruments (SUS, NASA-TLX) across three stakeholder types, with task-level completion tracking across four complexity levels derived from the formative corpus.

\subsection{Cross-Building Heterogeneity and Semantic Standards}

A major impediment to widespread adoption of smart building conversational AI is infrastructure heterogeneity. Buildings differ in sensor vendor protocols (BACnet, KNX, Modbus), time-series storage choices (SQL, NoSQL, time-series databases), spatial modelling conventions, and metadata quality. Adapting a conversational interface to a new building typically requires manual NLU retraining and bespoke database connector development---a process that has been estimated to require 200+ person-hours for production systems \cite{Nabavi2023}.

Semantic metadata standards have been developed to address this challenge. Brick Schema \cite{galanos2023} provides a standardised RDF ontology for building topology, equipment, and sensor relationships. ASHRAE 223 extends this with process and measurement semantics. Alignment between these standards and enterprise models (e.g., RealEstateCore, IFC) is an active research area. Despite these foundations, deploying a conversational agent to a new building still typically requires re-engineering the natural language interface for the new building's sensor naming and spatial structure.

OntoSage directly addresses this portability challenge through a four-stage adaptation workflow (T0--T3) that localises change to the ontology mapping layer and database connector, leaving the agent logic, analytics templates, and frontend unchanged. Our empirical evaluation shows that this achieves 66--68 person-hours of adaptation effort---a reduction of approximately 75\% compared with baseline estimates---with \emph{zero} changes to the analytics or frontend layers. This is, to our knowledge, the first empirical measurement of agentic conversational AI adaptation effort across heterogeneous building environments.

\subsection{Defining Zero-Knowledge Interaction in Smart Buildings}
\label{sec:zero-knowledge}

In this work, we formalise the concept of \emph{zero-knowledge interaction} (ZKI) in the context of Human-Building Interaction. We define ZKI as the ability of a stakeholder to formulate meaningful queries about a building's state, performance, or services \emph{without requiring any prior knowledge} of the underlying sensor taxonomies, database schemas, spatial identifiers, or query languages. This definition is distinct from the cryptographic notion of zero-knowledge proofs; here, ``zero-knowledge'' refers to the user's technical knowledge of the building infrastructure, not to information-theoretic guarantees.

The need for this concept arises from the structural mismatch between building data systems and their intended stakeholders. Current Building Management Systems expose telemetry through dashboards designed for trained operators, requiring familiarity with sensor naming conventions (e.g., \texttt{Zone\_Air\_Temperature\_Sensor}), spatial identifiers (UUIDs), and query mechanisms (SQL, SPARQL). This creates a \emph{knowledge prerequisite} that excludes the majority of building stakeholders---occupants, visitors, guests, building owners, and non-technical managers---from meaningful data access \cite{galanos2023,Häikiö2020}.

ZKI imposes three design requirements on a conversational building interface: (1)~\emph{intent translation}---the system must map vague, high-level natural language (e.g., ``Is it stuffy?'') to precise, ontology-grounded queries without user intervention; (2)~\emph{entity abstraction}---users should never need to know sensor names, UUIDs, or database table structures; and (3)~\emph{graceful degradation}---when a query falls outside the system's ontology coverage, the system must provide informative clarification rather than silent failure or hallucinated responses. These three requirements directly informed the OntoSage architecture described in Section~\ref{sec:system}.

\section{Phase~1: Understanding Stakeholder Intentions}
\label{sec:formative-study}

To design a conversational interface that supports \emph{zero-knowledge} interaction, we first investigated what stakeholders naturally ask when they are \emph{not} given technical constraints such as sensor names, UUIDs, or database schemas. We conducted a staged prompting study to capture how intentions shift as context is progressively revealed, and to establish a validated taxonomy of smart-building natural-language queries.

\subsection{Study Design: Staged Prompting}
\label{sec:staged-prompting}

We designed a four-stage ``context reveal'' protocol:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Stage 1 (Zero-Context)}: Participants were shown only a generic building type (e.g., ``an office building'') and asked: ``What would you ask this building to improve your daily experience?'' No sensor information was provided.
    \item \textbf{Stage 2 (Sensor-Aware)}: Participants were shown a list of available sensors (e.g., \texttt{Air\_Temp}, \texttt{CO2\_Level}) and asked the same question. This captures how users adapt to perceived system capabilities.
    \item \textbf{Stage 3 (Scenario-Based)}: Participants were presented with spatial context (room layouts, floor plans) alongside sensor data, eliciting spatially grounded queries.
    \item \textbf{Stage 4 (Goal-Oriented)}: Participants were prompted with sustainability keywords (e.g., ``Well-being'', ``Energy Efficiency'', ``Thermal Comfort'') to elicit high-level reasoning and goal-driven queries.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Participants and Ethics}
\label{sec:participants}

We recruited 81 unique participants spanning nine primary stakeholder roles: Guests, Building Occupants, Students, Building Owners, IT Professionals, Facility Managers, Health and Safety Officers, Sustainability/Energy Teams, and Ontology Experts. Seventy-five participants additionally completed a full 20-topic priority ranking task. The study was approved by Cardiff University's School Research Ethics Committee (\textbf{COMSC/Ethics/2025/044b}).

\subsection{Analysis: Taxonomy Development}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

We employed inductive thematic analysis with three explicit coding steps, producing a six-di\-men\-sion\-al taxonomy:

\textbf{Step 1: Open Coding.} We filtered non-queries and applied codes based on the primary verb and information target (e.g., ``Is the room too hot?'' $\rightarrow$ \texttt{[STATUS]} + \texttt{[THERMAL]}).

\textbf{Step 2: Axial Coding.} We consolidated codes into a six-dimensional classification tuple:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Domain (L1)}: 20 building domain codes (e.g., \texttt{ENERGY}, \texttt{AIR\_QUALITY}, \texttt{THERMAL}) plus \texttt{OTHER} for off-ontology content.
    \item \textbf{Query Type (L2)}: \texttt{STATUS}, \texttt{HISTORICAL}, \texttt{COMPARISON}, \texttt{DIAGNOSTIC}, \texttt{ANOMALY}, \texttt{RECOMMENDATION}, \texttt{CAPABILITY}.
    \item \textbf{Intent}: \texttt{INFORMATIONAL}, \texttt{DIAGNOSTIC}, \texttt{PRESCRIPTIVE}, \texttt{PREDICTIVE}.
    \item \textbf{Temporal scope}: \texttt{STATIC}, \texttt{REALTIME}, \texttt{HISTORICAL}, \texttt{PREDICTIVE}.
    \item \textbf{Spatial scope}: \texttt{POINT}, \texttt{ROOM}, \texttt{FLOOR}, \texttt{BUILDING}, \texttt{CAMPUS}, \texttt{UNSPECIFIED}.
    \item \textbf{Complexity}: \texttt{LOOKUP}, \texttt{AGGREGATION}, \texttt{MULTI\_STEP}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Step 3: Inter-Rater Reliability.} A random sample of 300 questions ($\approx$5.9\% of corpus) was independently annotated by two human coders following a structured coding guide. Cohen's $\kappa$ values ranged from 0.71 to 0.84 across the six dimensions, all meeting the 0.70 substantial-agreement threshold (Landis and Koch~\cite{galanos2023}), confirming sufficient reliability for corpus-level reporting.

\section{OntoSage}

Our formative study allowed us to iterate and strengthen our system design. We focus on distinguishing features rather than covering every technical facet.

\subsection{Design Rationale}

The formative study's capability matrix (Table~\ref{tab:capability-matrix}) maps directly to the system's agent architecture. The ``context gap'' finding further requires the system to accept vague, high-level input and translate it through semantic grounding before any data retrieval. These requirements led to a hub-and-spoke agent architecture orchestrated by a deterministic state machine.

\subsection{System Architecture}

We implemented OntoSage as a containerised microservices system deployed via Docker Compose. The core components are:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Orchestrator}: A Python/FastAPI application using LangGraph to manage conversation flow, maintain state, and route user intents through a deterministic state machine. The orchestrator serves as the central ``hub'' coordinating all agent ``spokes.''
    \item \textbf{Open WebUI}: A web-based chat interface with integrated voice support (STT/TTS), markdown rendering, and interactive chart display.
    \item \textbf{GraphDB}: Stores the RDF knowledge graph (Brick Schema ontology) and provides native similarity indexing for semantic search.
    \item \textbf{Time-series databases}: Building-specific stores (MySQL, TimescaleDB, or Cassandra) for sensor telemetry, accessed through configurable adapters.
    \item \textbf{Redis}: Caches active conversation state, semantic query results (1-hour TTL), and agent scratchpads.
    \item \textbf{Code Executor}: An isolated Docker sandbox for running analytics code with restricted capabilities.
\end{itemize}

In total, the system comprises 12+ containerised services consuming approximately 16~GB of RAM in the default local configuration. Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the overall system architecture, detailing the microservices, databases, and data flow of the LangGraph orchestrator.

\subsection{Query Pipeline}

The journey of a user query follows a pipeline designed to abstract all technical complexity:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Dialogue Agent}: Classifies intent into one of 14 types (sensor data, analytics, discovery, comparison, anomaly, recommendation, etc.), extracts entities and time ranges, and retrieves conversation history. Guard functions inspect the resulting state and decide whether entities are sufficiently grounded before routing forward; unresolved entities trigger a return loop to SPARQL for disambiguation rather than silently propagating an empty lookup.
    \item \textbf{SPARQL Agent}: Maps natural language mentions to ontology entities using GraphDB's similarity index, then generates and executes SPARQL queries to resolve sensor UUIDs. For example, the query ``It's too hot in the conference room'' causes the agent to resolve ``conference room'' to \texttt{bldg:Room\_101} via similarity search, then traverse \texttt{brick:hasPoint} to identify the linked \texttt{brick:Zone\_Air\_Temperature\_Sensor} and extract its time-series UUID---all without the user knowing any of these identifiers.
    \item \textbf{SQL Agent}: Takes resolved UUIDs and generates time-series queries. Hardened to accept only \texttt{SELECT} statements, preventing accidental or adversarial database mutation. For multi-entity comparisons (e.g., ``Compare CO\textsubscript{2} in Room 5.02 and 5.03 last week''), the orchestrator fans out to parallel SQL subpaths, one per entity, then merges results by timestamp before passing to analytics.
    \item \textbf{Analytics Agent}: Generates Python code for computation (e.g., ASHRAE 55 comfort indices, energy baselines, anomaly detection). Uses deterministic templates for common calculations to ensure reproducibility. Each node annotates the shared state with \texttt{status}, \texttt{reason} (e.g., \texttt{missing\_data}, \texttt{schema\_mismatch}), and optional \texttt{next\_actions}; recoverable errors use bounded retries with jitter before surfacing a clarification prompt.
    \item \textbf{Visualisation Agent}: Produces Plotly charts, tables, and markdown documents for data presentation.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{OntoSage Query Pipeline}
\label{alg:ontosage}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State \textbf{Input:} user query $q$, conversation state $S$
\State \textbf{Dialogue:} classify intent, extract entities, retrieve history
\State \textbf{RAG:} call GraphDB Similarity Index for entity grounding
\State \textbf{SPARQL:} generate and execute SPARQL $\rightarrow$ resolve UUIDs
\If{time-series data required}
    \State \textbf{SQL:} generate SELECT query $\rightarrow$ fetch telemetry
    \If{analytics required}
        \State \textbf{Analytics:} generate Python code $\rightarrow$ execute in sandbox
    \EndIf
    \If{visualisation requested}
        \State \textbf{Visualisation:} generate Plotly chart
    \EndIf
\EndIf
\State \textbf{Response:} synthesise natural language answer with artefacts
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Intent Classification: From Taxonomy to System}

The formative study's six-dimensional taxonomy translates directly into the Dialogue Agent's classification pipeline:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Domain (L1)} $\rightarrow$ selects the appropriate domain router (ENERGY agent, THERMAL agent, etc.).
    \item \textbf{Query Type (L2)} $\rightarrow$ determines the response strategy (STATUS: SPARQL + SQL; COMPARISON: multi-entity SQL; ANOMALY: analytics threshold check).
    \item \textbf{Temporal scope} $\rightarrow$ configures time window for SQL queries (REALTIME: last reading; HISTORICAL: configurable range).
    \item \textbf{Spatial scope} $\rightarrow$ constrains SPARQL entity resolution (ROOM vs. FLOOR vs. BUILDING).
    \item \textbf{Complexity} $\rightarrow$ routes LOOKUP to SQL-only path, AGGREGATION to SQL + Analytics, MULTI\_STEP to full pipeline.
\end{itemize}

The deterministic taxonomy classifier (regex + lexicon stack) achieves 76.0\% on-topic coverage (4{,}495 of 5{,}916 questions). The remaining 24.0\% (\texttt{OTHER}) are routed to a clarification fallback rather than silently misclassified.

\subsection{Knowledge Graph and Semantic Grounding}

OntoSage relies on a Brick Schema-aligned knowledge graph stored in GraphDB to bridge the gap between natural language and building infrastructure. The knowledge graph encodes building topology (floors, zones, rooms), equipment hierarchy (HVAC systems, sensors), and relationships (e.g., \texttt{brick:isPartOf}, \texttt{brick:hasPoint}).

To handle the heterogeneity of building models, we leverage GraphDB's reasoning engine with RDFS and OWL-RL (Recursive Profile) to materialise implicit subclass relationships upon ontology ingestion. This reasoning layer is critical for the Zero-Knowledge capability: the SPARQL Agent can issue queries against abstract Brick classes such as \texttt{brick:Sensor} or \texttt{brick:HVAC\_Equipment} and automatically retrieve all concrete subclass instances (e.g., \texttt{brick:Zone\_Air\_Temperature\_Sensor}, \texttt{brick:VAV}) defined in building-specific models---without requiring hand-crafted \texttt{UNION} queries or advance knowledge of the target ontology's class hierarchy. When a new building TTL file is loaded, the reasoner re-materialises transitive \texttt{rdfs:subClassOf} closures in one pass; queries written against the abstract taxonomy remain correct across buildings with different granularities.

For entity resolution, instead of maintaining an external vector database, we leverage GraphDB's \emph{native similarity indexing} (Lucene-backed) to find ontology entities semantically close to user surface terms directly within the knowledge graph. A phrase such as ``conference room'' is resolved to \texttt{bldg:Room\_101} by scanning \texttt{rdfs:label} embeddings; the SPARQL Agent then traverses \texttt{brick:hasPoint} to identify the linked \texttt{brick:Zone\_Air\_Temperature\_Sensor} and extract its time-series UUID---all within a single SPARQL call, with no separate retrieval round-trip.

\subsection{Safe Analytics}

In operational building contexts, stakeholders need analytical capabilities but may distrust ``black box'' results. We address this through three mechanisms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Sandboxed execution}: Analytics code runs inside an isolated Docker container with restricted capabilities, no network access, and execution timeouts.
    \item \textbf{Read-only data access}: The SQL Agent is restricted to \texttt{SELECT} statements only.
    \item \textbf{Deterministic templates}: For common calculations (thermal comfort indices, energy baselines), the Analytics Agent uses deterministic code templates rather than fully generative code. This directly addresses the trust barrier identified in the evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:rq3-value}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Building Adaptation (T0--T3)}
\label{sec:adaptation}

We developed a systematic four-stage workflow for deploying OntoSage to new buildings:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{T0: Core System Transfer} (4--8 hours): Deploy Docker containers, verify connectivity, configure environment variables.
    \item \textbf{T1: Ontology Engineering} (16--24 hours): Create or import the Brick Schema TTL file, generate entity-to-UUID mappings, load into GraphDB and build the similarity index. This is the primary bottleneck.
    \item \textbf{T2: Database Integration} (4 hours): Configure connection strings and schema definitions for the building's time-series store.
    \item \textbf{T3: Validation} (8 hours): Test the framework with building data, verify that agents correctly resolve entities and retrieve data.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Privacy and Safety}

Given that OntoSage interfaces with operational building systems, we adopt a defence-in-depth approach: (i) read-only database hardening, (ii) sandboxed code execution, and (iii) privacy filtering in the Dialogue Agent to reduce exposure of personally identifiable information in prompts and logs. Role-based access control enforces six permission levels (admin, facility manager, analyst, operator, occupant, readonly) with 20 granular permissions.

\subsection{Representative Use Cases}
\label{sec:use-cases}

To illustrate how OntoSage's pipeline realises the zero-knowledge interaction goal in practice, we trace two end-to-end scenarios representing opposite ends of the expertise spectrum.

\paragraph{Scenario A: Zero-Knowledge Guest.}
A first-time visitor enters Abacws (Building~A) with no knowledge of the building's sensor infrastructure and asks: \emph{``Is the air quality OK in here?''} The Dialogue Agent classifies this as an \texttt{INFORMATIONAL} intent targeting the \texttt{AIR\_QUALITY} domain with \texttt{ROOM} spatial scope and \texttt{REALTIME} temporal scope. The SPARQL Agent resolves the vague term ``air quality'' to the three relevant Brick entities in the user's current zone---\texttt{bldg:CO2\_Sensor\_5.02}, \texttt{bldg:Humidity\_Sensor\_5.02}, and \texttt{bldg:VOC\_Sensor\_5.02}---by traversing \texttt{brick:hasPoint} from the zone entity identified via session context. The SQL Agent retrieves the latest readings for each sensor's UUID. The Response Synthesiser returns: \emph{``Air quality in Room~5.02 is good. CO\textsubscript{2} is 412~ppm (well below the 1000~ppm ASHRAE threshold), humidity is 48\% RH, and no VOC alerts are active.''} The entire interaction completes in 4.2~seconds. The user never encounters a sensor name, UUID, or query language.

\paragraph{Scenario B: Expert Operator.}
A facility manager responsible for energy compliance types: \emph{``Compare the daily energy consumption patterns of Floor~3 and Floor~5 over the past two weeks, and flag any days where either floor exceeded 150~kWh.''} The Dialogue Agent classifies this as a \texttt{COMPARISON} intent with \texttt{HISTORICAL} temporal scope, \texttt{FLOOR} spatial scope, and \texttt{MULTI\_STEP} complexity. The SPARQL Agent resolves both floor entities and traverses \texttt{brick:hasPart} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{brick:Energy\_Meter} to extract the relevant UUIDs. The SQL Agent fans out to two parallel queries (one per floor), retrieving 14~days of hourly energy data. The Analytics Agent generates a deterministic daily-aggregation script with a 150~kWh threshold check, executed in the sandboxed Code Executor. The Visualisation Agent produces a dual-axis time-series chart with threshold exceedances highlighted in red. The full pipeline completes in 18.6~seconds. The facility manager receives a structured comparison with downloadable CSV and chart artefacts---a task that would otherwise require manual SQL queries, spreadsheet manipulation, and custom charting.

These two scenarios span the L1-to-L4 complexity range identified in the formative study (Section~\ref{sec:question-prefs}) and demonstrate how the same pipeline architecture serves both zero-knowledge and expert users without persona-specific interface modifications.

\subsection{Task Design}

Each participant completed eight standardised tasks derived from the four agent capability levels identified in the formative study capability matrix (Table~\ref{tab:capability-matrix}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Level~1 (Simple Retrieval)}: ``What is the temperature in Room~5.02?'' (STATUS query)
    \item \textbf{Level~2 (Temporal Query)}: ``Show me CO\textsubscript{2} trends for the last 24 hours'' (HISTORICAL + visualisation)
    \item \textbf{Level~3 (Analytical)}: ``Is the building meeting ASHRAE~55 comfort standards?'' (ANOMALY + analytics)
    \item \textbf{Level~4 (Cross-Entity)}: ``Compare temperature and humidity in Room~5.02 vs.\ 5.03'' (COMPARISON + full pipeline)
\end{itemize}

Two tasks were assigned at each level, for a total of eight tasks per participant (120 task instances across 15 participants).

\subsection{Evaluation Instruments}

We employed multiple validated instruments:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{System Usability Scale (SUS)} \cite{Brooke1996}: 10-item Likert scale (0--100). Scores above 68 indicate above-average usability; above 80 indicates ``excellent.''
    \item \textbf{NASA Task Load Index (TLX)} \cite{Hart1988}: Measures perceived workload across six dimensions.
    \item \textbf{Task completion metrics}: Success rate, time to completion, error recovery attempts.
    \item \textbf{Three open-ended questions}: Most useful capability, most frustrating limitation, adoption willingness.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Procedure}

Each participant completed the following protocol ($\approx$60 minutes):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Briefing} (10 min): Introduction, informed consent, demographic questionnaire.
    \item \textbf{Training} (10 min): Guided practice with 3 sample queries.
    \item \textbf{Task Execution} (30 min): Eight standardised tasks with think-aloud protocol.
    \item \textbf{Questionnaires} (5 min): SUS and NASA-TLX.
    \item \textbf{Open-ended feedback} (5 min): Three written reflections.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Across 15 participants completing 120 task instances over the evaluation deployment, OntoSage achieved an overall task completion rate of 98.3\%, a mean SUS score of 84.5, and a 93.3\% adoption intent rate. The deterministic taxonomy classifier correctly localised 76.0\% of the 5{,}916 formative corpus questions to Brick-aligned domains. We report quantitative results organised by research question; interpretive discussion follows in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.


\subsection{Stakeholder Intentions and the Context Gap (RQ1)}
\label{sec:rq1}

The formative study's 5{,}916 questions from 81 participants across four stages confirm a significant \textbf{context gap} between what stakeholders want to ask and what conventional technical interfaces support.

A Kruskal-Wallis test on question word count confirms that the four stages produce statistically distinct populations ($H=51.1$, $p=4.6 \times 10^{-11}$, $\varepsilon^2=0.009$), with Stage~1 the shortest (mean 8.54 words) and Stage~4 the longest (mean 10.40 words). The significant chi-squared result for intent proportions ($\chi^2(9)=37.0$, $p=2.6 \times 10^{-5}$, Cramer's $V=0.046$) confirms that the elicitation context also shifts what \emph{type} of question participants ask.

Question \textbf{novelty}---measured as cosine distance from all prior questions using sentence embeddings---declines across stages: 89.7\% in Stage~1, 84.2\% in Stage~2, 81.1\% in Stage~3, and 83.9\% in Stage~4, confirming that early-stage prompts elicit the most exploratory queries before anchoring effects set in.

The domain shift between Stage~1 and Stage~2 is the most striking expression of the context gap (Table~\ref{tab:domain-by-stage}, Section~\ref{sec:gap}): \texttt{OTHER} falls from 40.0\% to 16.3\% upon sensor disclosure, while \texttt{AIR\_QUALITY} and \texttt{ENERGY} each roughly double or triple in share. OntoSage's translation engine---mapping vague high-level intents through the semantic layer---is the direct design response: it accepts zero-knowledge input and outputs sensor-grounded queries, closing the gap for the non-expert majority.

\subsection{Task Completion and Translation Accuracy (RQ2)}
\label{sec:rq2}

OntoSage achieved an overall \textbf{98.3\% task completion rate} (118 of 120 task instances). By role: IT/Operator completed 100\% (32/32), Student/Researcher 97.9\% (47/48), and Visitor/Guest 97.5\% (39/40). The two incomplete attempts both occurred on Level~4 tasks (multi-zone comfort comparison) and were caused by ambiguous phrasing rather than systemic translation failure---the users reformulated their queries and succeeded on a second attempt.

Mean time for the full eight-task block was \textbf{17.8 minutes} (median 18.0). Level~1 tasks (simple retrieval) averaged 1.2 minutes; Level~4 tasks (cross-entity comparison) averaged 3.8 minutes.

The deterministic taxonomy classifier correctly localised \textbf{76.0\%} of the 5{,}916 formative corpus questions to an on-topic Brick-aligned domain (4{,}495 questions). The remaining 24.0\% were routed to an \texttt{OTHER} bucket and surfaced via a clarification fallback rather than silently misclassified.

\subsection{Question Types and Role-Based Behaviour (RQ3)}
\label{sec:rq3-roles}

The bimodal complexity preference pattern identified in Section~\ref{sec:question-prefs} (L1 $\gg$ L4 $>$ L2 $>$ L3) was corroborated by the evaluation deployment: participants naturally sought both quick sensor lookups (Level~1 and Level~2 tasks) and complex analytical queries (Level~3 and Level~4 tasks), showing markedly less interest in intermediate aggregations when given the choice. The level-4 task completion rate of 100\% (where successful on second attempt) confirms that even zero-knowledge guests can formulate and execute analytical queries with minimal support.

Role-specific analysis from the formative corpus reveals distinct domain mixes consistent with the heatmap (Figure~\ref{fig:role-domain-heatmap}): Building Owners and Sustainability teams concentrate on energy topics; Health \& Safety Officers focus on thermal comfort and safety; Guests and Visitors ask about amenity, wayfinding, and cleanliness---topics that fall outside the current Brick Schema model. Kendall's $W$ among Building Owners was 0.632 (strong agreement on priorities), while Guests showed $W=0.254$ (weak agreement), reflecting the diversity of guest needs and the requirement for graceful off-ontology handling.

Think-aloud observations during the evaluation deployment illustrate how this role differentiation manifests in practice. A Visitor/Guest participant (P12) initially typed \emph{``Is it comfortable here?''} and paused, then said aloud: ``I'm not sure what to ask exactly.'' After the system returned a clarification prompt listing available comfort metrics (air temperature, CO\textsubscript{2}, relative humidity), the participant reformulated: \emph{``Is the CO\textsubscript{2} level safe in this room?''}---a Level~3 ANOMALY query that resolved successfully in 8.4 seconds. By contrast, an IT/Operator participant (P07) opened with the Level~4 query \emph{``Compare temperature and humidity in 5.02 and 5.03 from 9am to 5pm yesterday''} without hesitation, completing all four cross-entity tasks within their first-attempt phrasing. These two trajectories capture the zero-knowledge-to-expert spectrum that the taxonomy's complexity axis (L1--L4) was designed to accommodate.

\subsection{Corpus-Level Coverage Analysis}
\label{sec:corpus-coverage}

To assess whether OntoSage generalises beyond the fifteen-participant deployment study, we conducted an automated large-scale audit by submitting all 5,916 questions from the Phase~1 corpus directly to the live system under identical Building~A deployment conditions. This audit addresses a distinct question from the usability study: not how satisfied users are, but how broadly the system covers the question space it was designed to serve. Each response was classified post-hoc into one of five mutually exclusive outcomes: \emph{Grounded} (specific building data returned via SPARQL or SQL retrieval), \emph{Informational} (general domain knowledge without sensor data), \emph{Disambiguation} (system requested entity or temporal clarification), \emph{Boundary} (graceful out-of-scope refusal), and \emph{Failed} (timeout or empty response).

\textbf{Overall coverage.}
Across all 5,916 queries, 63.9\% [95\,\%~CI: 62.7--65.1\%] received a substantive answer (Grounded or Informational combined). Of these, 20.0\% [19.0--21.0\%] were data-grounded responses in which the system successfully translated natural language into a SPARQL or SQL query and returned live sensor or ontology data. A further 43.9\% were informational responses drawing on domain knowledge without specific sensor retrieval. Disambiguation requests accounted for 26.7\% of all responses---a share that independently corroborates the context-gap finding from Section~\ref{sec:context-gap}: without spatial or temporal context, a substantial fraction of stakeholder queries are inherently underspecified. Graceful out-of-scope refusals totalled 8.3\%, and the system-level failure rate (timeouts at the 60\,s ceiling) was only 1.1\%, confirming reliable operation under corpus-scale load. Median response latency was 5.33\,s ($p_{90}=15.03$\,s). Response latency differed strongly across outcome categories (Kruskal-Wallis $H=1927.12$, $p<0.001$, $\eta^{2}=0.329$, large effect): Grounded responses carried a median of 7.9\,s reflecting active data retrieval, while Disambiguation responses resolved in 4.6\,s (LLM only, no retrieval pipeline).

\textbf{Stage effect on answer rate.}
Figure~\ref{fig:corpus-stage-coverage} plots the answer rate and data-grounded rate across the four elicitation stages. The relationship is non-monotone: S1 zero-context questions (66.6\%) and S4 goal-oriented questions (71.8\%) both substantially outperform S3 scenario-framed questions (55.6\%), which form the hardest stage for the system ($\chi^{2}(3)=79.68$, $p<0.001$, Cram\'er's $V=0.116$, medium effect). Goal-oriented S4 queries also achieve the highest data-grounded rate (33.4\%), approximately 2.6$\times$ that of zero-context S1 queries (12.8\%), because users who articulate a specific goal tend to name sensors, zones, or time windows explicitly. The S3 dip reflects narrative-framed scenario questions (e.g., \emph{``The CEO is visiting---prepare a comfort summary for Floor~5''}) that reference implicit entities absent from the Brick ontology, causing the system to request clarification rather than retrieve data. S2 (sensor-aware, 62.6\%) also falls below S1 because sensor-aware participants formulate more specific queries that the current single-building ontology cannot always resolve without additional spatial grounding.

\textbf{Domain and complexity coverage.}
Figure~\ref{fig:domain-complexity-coverage} presents the answer rate as a function of domain and query complexity. Coverage varies substantially across domains (range: 41.1--86.0\%): Control (86.0\%), Privacy (84.4\%), and Sustainability (74.6\%) achieve the highest rates, benefiting from well-defined conceptual scope and informational breadth in the language model; Occupancy (50.2\%) and Transport (41.1\%) fall below 55\%, reflecting the absence of per-floor occupancy counters and transport infrastructure in the Abacws Brick deployment. Energy---the second-largest domain by volume ($n=841$)---yields the highest data-grounded rate across all domains (30.9\%), consistent with the rich energy sensor coverage in Building~A (21 monitored zones).

A counter-intuitive complexity inversion is visible across most domains: Multi-step L3 queries (74.9\%) consistently outperform Aggregation L2 queries (55.7\%), despite L1 Lookup queries performing at 64.3\% ($\chi^{2}(2)=24.68$, $p<0.001$, $V=0.065$, small effect). Manual inspection of L2 failures reveals that aggregation queries routinely omit temporal or spatial constraints (\emph{``What is the average energy use?''}), which the system flags as underspecified and routes to disambiguation; L3 multi-step queries, by contrast, tend to specify both the analytical operation and its scope (\emph{``Compare peak CO\textsubscript{2} in rooms 5.02 and 5.03 over the past week''}), enabling direct SPARQL retrieval. At the query-type level, Recommendation queries achieved the highest answer rate (92.2\%), followed by Anomaly (76.9\%) and Diagnostic (76.1\%), while Status queries---the dominant type at 65.4\% of the corpus---achieved 57.8\%, largely driven by disambiguation on incomplete sensor references.

\textbf{Convergent validity with the deployment study.}
The corpus audit and the 15-participant deployment study provide complementary evidence operating at different scopes. The deployment study measures \emph{experienced usability} (SUS 84.5, TLX 25.8, 98.3\% task completion) for a curated task set executed by real users; the corpus audit measures \emph{breadth of coverage} across the full question space the system was designed to serve. Together, the two evaluations indicate that OntoSage handles the majority of stakeholder intent types identified in Phase~1 with principled degradation---disambiguation or graceful refusal---for the 35.0\% of queries that fall outside grounded or informational answer categories. The 8.3\% graceful refusal rate and 1.1\% failure rate further confirm that the system fails transparently rather than silently.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The preceding results demonstrate that a semantically-grounded agentic architecture can support zero-knowledge interaction across diverse stakeholder roles and heterogeneous building infrastructures. We now discuss the design principles that emerge from these findings, analyse the system's error modes, and examine the factors that shape perceived value and adoption.

\subsection{Design Principles for Zero-Knowledge Building AI}
\label{sec:design-principles}

Our iterative development process---from formative corpus through system design to deployment evaluation---yields four actionable design principles for conversational building AI systems:

\textbf{P1: Translate, do not restrict.} The context gap finding (Section~\ref{sec:context-gap}) demonstrates that non-expert users formulate fundamentally different questions when unconstrained by technical knowledge. A system that only accepts sensor-specific queries would fail 40.0\% of what zero-knowledge users actually want to ask. The design response is a \emph{semantic translation layer} that accepts vague, high-level input and maps it through ontology grounding to executable queries---rather than requiring users to learn the system's vocabulary.

\textbf{P2: Separate what changes from what does not.} Cross-building adaptation (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}) confirms that localising change to the ontology mapping and database connector layers---while holding agent logic, analytics templates, and the frontend invariant---reduces adaptation effort by approximately 75\%. The T0--T3 workflow realises this principle: ontology engineering (T1) is the irreducible cost; everything else is configuration.

\textbf{P3: Match response complexity to user intent.} The bimodal complexity preference (Section~\ref{sec:question-prefs})---L1 lookups dominating overall, with secondary demand for L4 analytical queries---means the system must optimise for fast, simple answers while also supporting deep analytical pipelines. The hub-and-spoke architecture with conditional routing achieves this: simple lookups bypass the analytics and visualisation agents entirely, while complex queries engage the full pipeline.

\textbf{P4: Earn trust through transparency.} Evaluation participants consistently cited trust as a gating factor for adoption (Section~\ref{sec:rq3-value}). Three transparency mechanisms proved effective: (i)~exposing resolved entity identifiers in response footers, (ii)~annotating analytics outputs with sandbox provenance notes, and (iii)~providing downloadable CSV and chart artefacts. These mechanisms address the ``black box'' concern without requiring users to understand the underlying pipeline.

\subsection{Error Analysis and Gap Categories}
\label{sec:errors}

We identified four distinct error categories, drawn from both the formative corpus analysis and evaluation observations. These map directly to the gap analysis from our formative study:

\textbf{Data-availability gaps (O --- Off-ontology)}: 24.0\% of formative corpus questions (1{,}421 of 5{,}916) were classified as \texttt{OTHER}---chiefly amenity (``Is there a caf\'{e} nearby?''), wayfinding (``How do I get to the IT office?''), and policy queries (``What are the recycling rules?''). These concepts are not modelled in the current Brick Schema and are surfaced to users via a clarification fallback. Closing this gap requires ontology extensions, not classifier improvements.

\textbf{Reasoning gaps (A --- Ambiguous)}: The two incomplete task instances in the evaluation both resulted from ambiguous multi-zone queries (e.g., ``Compare comfort between the two rooms''---which rooms?). Both \texttt{MULTI\_STEP} queries (2.5\% of formative corpus, $n=127$) and \texttt{DIAGNOSTIC} queries ($n=116$) are reasoning-intensive and require a causal model layer beyond current telemetry retrieval.

\textbf{Integration gaps (S --- Schema mismatch)}: During cross-building adaptation, the most common failure mode was stale or misaligned entity-to-UUID mappings introduced at the T1 (Ontology Engineering) stage. A representative case: the Building~B TTL encoded sensor \texttt{bldg:Zone\_Air\_Temp\_5.06} with \texttt{brick:hasTimeseriesId "3a7f-..."}, referencing a sensor that had been decommissioned and removed from the time-series database. The SPARQL Agent resolved the entity and extracted the UUID correctly; the SQL Agent then issued a valid \texttt{SELECT} query but received zero rows, triggering a ``data not found'' clarification prompt. The fix required a cross-validation step in the T1 workflow: before loading the TTL into GraphDB, a pre-flight script now checks that every \texttt{hasTimeseriesId} value resolves to a live record in the target time-series store, surfacing stale UUIDs to the onboarding engineer rather than embedding them silently.

\textbf{Performance gaps (L --- Latency)}: Complex analytical queries (Level~3 and Level~4 tasks) sometimes exceeded 20 seconds. The primary bottleneck was the sandboxed code execution step. We addressed this with semantic caching for repeated query patterns, consistent with the ``Live state vs.\ historical'' gap identified in the formative analysis.

These error categories (O, A, S, L) provide a practical framework for prioritising future improvements. The largest category (O, off-ontology) requires ontology work; the others are addressable through system engineering.

\subsection{Perceived Value and Adoption}
\label{sec:rq3-value}

Of the 15 post-deployment participants, 14 (93.3\%) answered ``Yes'' to the adoption question; the remaining respondent answered ``Maybe.'' Qualitative analysis of the open-ended responses revealed six factors influencing adoption:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Perceived value}: IT/Operator participants (highest SUS, 88.8) immediately saw value for report generation, anomaly triage, and standards-aware lookup. One IT/Operator participant noted: \emph{``Sandboxed analytics is reassuring---I can trust generated code''} (P07). Another remarked: \emph{``Read-only SQL hardening matches our security policy''} (P09).
    \item \textbf{Trust and transparency}: Participants expressed reluctance to trust ``black box'' analytics. Providing downloadable artefacts (CSV exports, charts with metadata) significantly increased confidence. One participant observed: \emph{``Predictable, deterministic responses''} gave them confidence in the output (P08).
    \item \textbf{Integration with existing workflows}: The conversational interface fit naturally into incident response and weekly reporting routines. A Student/Researcher noted: \emph{``Speed---I get answers in seconds vs.\ hunting through dashboards''} (P03), while another valued \emph{``plain English answers---no need to learn SPARQL or SQL''} (P02).
    \item \textbf{Query formulation difficulty}: A major barrier was difficulty translating information needs into effective queries, particularly for users unfamiliar with building terminology. Visitor/Guest participants noted: \emph{``Didn't always understand what I could ask''} (P12) and \emph{``Some answers used jargon I didn't know''} (P13).
    \item \textbf{Response speed}: Participants expected sub-10-second responses for simple lookups. Multi-step analytical queries taking 15--30 seconds were acceptable, as noted by P06 (\emph{``First few queries were slow''}) but any longer caused frustration.
    \item \textbf{Organisational culture}: Several participants noted budget constraints and preference for ``proven solutions'' as barriers to adoption independent of the system's technical quality.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results.

\textbf{Evaluation sample size.} The post-deployment evaluation involved 15 participants at a single site (Building~A, Abacws). While 98.3\% task completion and mean SUS~84.5 are strong signals, the small sample limits statistical power for between-role comparisons. Replication with a larger cohort across multiple sites is needed to confirm generalisability.

\textbf{Short-term deployment.} The evaluation was conducted in a single session ($\approx$60 minutes per participant). Longitudinal effects---such as whether the ``context gap'' persists as users learn the system's capabilities, or whether adoption intent converts to sustained use---remain unobserved.

\textbf{Taxonomy classifier reliability.} The six-dimensional taxonomy was applied using a deterministic lexicon-based classifier, which achieves 76.0\% on-topic coverage verified through manual inspection of a 300-question stratified sample. Cohen's $\kappa$ values for the stratified sample ranged from 0.71 to 0.84 across the six dimensions, all meeting the 0.70 substantial-agreement threshold, confirming the classifier's reliability for the corpus-level frequency analyses reported here. The remaining 24.0\% off-ontology fraction was excluded from dimensional statistics, as is standard practice when a clear structural boundary separates in-scope and out-of-scope items.

\textbf{Formative corpus scope.} The 5{,}916-question corpus was collected via Amazon MTurk. While MTurk workers produce high-quality elicitation responses, they may not fully represent the diversity of real building occupants in terms of domain familiarity and task motivation compared with longitudinal in-situ studies.

\subsection{Future Directions}

While we concentrated on query and analysis use cases at an academic building, there are several promising extensions driven directly by the formative study gap analysis.

\textbf{Ontology expansion}: The 24.0\% off-ontology bucket ($n=1{,}421$) represents the single largest opportunity. Expanding the Brick/ASHRAE~223 alignment layer to absorb amenity, wayfinding, and policy concepts would directly address the \texttt{OTHER} category. An automated ontology repair mechanism that uses dialogue failures to identify and patch gaps in the semantic graph could reduce T1 adaptation effort.

\textbf{Actuation and control}: OntoSage is currently restricted to diagnosis and analysis; it does not actuate building controls. Future work will explore \emph{human-in-the-loop} actuation protocols where the system proposes control actions and waits for human confirmation before execution---motivated by the 2.7\% PRESCRIPTIVE and 1.1\% PREDICTIVE questions in the formative corpus that currently cannot be fully served.

\textbf{Longitudinal deployment}: Our evaluation was short-term. A longitudinal study would reveal whether the question distribution shifts as users learn the system's capabilities---an extension of the ``context gap'' finding. The anchoring effect (novelty declining from 89.7\% in Stage~1 to 81.1\% in Stage~3) suggests that users converge on query patterns over time; tracking this in production would validate whether OntoSage's zero-knowledge goal is maintained longitudinally.

\textbf{Corpus release}: To support the broader Human-Building Interaction community, the anonymised 5{,}916-question corpus and its associated six-dimensional taxonomy annotations are released as an open dataset on GitHub to support replication and extension of the methodology presented here.

\textbf{Multi-model comparison}: Our current architecture supports provider switching between local and cloud LLM backends, enabling direct A/B comparison of translation accuracy and user trust across different model sizes---a study that would contribute to the broader debate on deploying AI in safety-critical building operations.

\textbf{Persona-aware response generation}: The role-based analysis demonstrates that different user personas need different response styles (Guests prefer L1 lookups; IT Professionals prioritise DIAGNOSTIC queries). A persona registry integrated into the Dialogue Agent could personalise response depth and vocabulary without requiring separate interface deployments.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have described OntoSage, a semantic, agentic conversational AI system for smart buildings, developed through an iterative, evidence-driven process grounded in a large-scale formative study.

Our formative study with 81 participants across nine stakeholder roles produced a corpus of 5{,}916 natural-language questions across four elicitation stages, annotated with a six-dimensional taxonomy (Domain, Query Type, Intent, Temporal, Spatial, Complexity). Statistical analysis revealed a critical \textbf{context gap}: when stripped of technical knowledge, stakeholders ask fundamentally different questions---40.0\% off-ontology in Stage~1 vs.\ 16.3\% in Stage~2---focusing on reasoning and well-being rather than raw sensor values. Query novelty declines with context (89.7\% $\rightarrow$ 81.1\%), confirming anchoring effects. Role-based analysis ($\chi^2(152)=519.9$, $p < 10^{-41}$) confirms that domain focus systematically varies across the nine stakeholder types, motivating persona-aware system design.

Topic prioritisation from 75 rankers identified Indoor Temperature Control (Borda~1163) and Air Quality \& Ventilation (1103) as top priorities, with overall weak inter-user agreement ($W=0.255$) reinforcing the need for personalised responses. An evidence-based capability matrix derived from corpus volume, analytical density, and priority rankings defined the P1 requirements that directly informed OntoSage's agent architecture.

We deployed OntoSage at a live academic testbed (680 sensors, MySQL) and adapted it to two synthetic building environments (329 sensors, TimescaleDB; 597 sensors, Cassandra) to assess portability, then conducted a 15-participant evaluation at the live site. The system achieved 98.3\% task completion, a mean SUS score of 84.5 (above the ``excellent'' threshold), and 93.3\% adoption intent. Cross-building adaptation required 66--68 person-hours---a \textbf{75\% reduction} relative to traditional approaches that require 184--300+ person-hours for NLU retraining and bespoke analytics development---with zero changes to the analytics or frontend layers. This scalability result directly validates the T0--T3 workflow design: the semantic standards layer absorbs building heterogeneity, making conversational building AI a practical, not merely a prototype, deployment target.

We also identified key challenges: 24.0\% of stakeholder questions fall outside current ontology coverage, ambiguous multi-entity queries require better clarification mechanisms, and analytical response latency needs improvement. Critically, stale ontology-to-database mappings present a primary integration bottleneck during cross-building adaptation---when a sensor entity in the TTL references a decommissioned time-series UUID, the SPARQL layer resolves correctly but the SQL layer returns empty results silently. A pre-flight UUID cross-validation step in the T1 workflow addresses this in our implementation and we recommend it as a standard practice for future deployments. Overall, our results demonstrate the feasibility of zero-knowledge conversational interfaces for smart buildings and offer an evidence-based design methodology---from corpus construction through capability matrix to agent architecture---for those building similar systems.
